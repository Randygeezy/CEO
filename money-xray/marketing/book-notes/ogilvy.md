## 1. Core principles

**Purpose.** Advertising is information, not entertainment or art. Judge it only by whether it sells (pp. 8, 102, 133). Ogilvy cites John Caples: one ad sold 19½ times as much as another for the same product. The appeal is what changed (pp. 102, 134).

**Homework, positioning, image.**
- Know the product and the buyer before writing. Without that you get "irrelevant brilliance" (pp. 12, 103).
- Positioning means saying what the product does and who it is for (p. 135).
- Every ad adds to one consistent brand personality: name, packaging, price and the style of the ads (p. 103).
- Promise one important benefit, as P&G does (pp. 74, 119).
- If competitors are also good, don't imply you are better. Show that you are "positively good" (pp. 104, 137).
- Keep winning ads running. Repetition does not wear out readership (p. 14).

**Headlines** (ch. 7; pp. 39-40, 110, 119, 157-158)
- Five times as many people read the headline as the body (pp. 39, 110).
- Headlines that promise a benefit are read by about four times as many people (p. 119). Headlines with news are recalled by 22% more people (p. 110).
- Put the brand name in the headline (p. 110).
- Name the audience (p. 158). Exact figures beat vague claims; long headlines can sell more (p. 40).

**Body copy and long copy**
- "The more facts you tell, the more you sell" (p. 110). Long copy outsells short for considered purchases (pp. 158, 177-178).
- You cannot bore people into buying (p. 110).
- Write to one person: short sentences, no jargon, subheads, story (p. 160).

**Testimonials.** Real users, speaking naturally, build credibility. Celebrities pull attention away from the product and often don't change brand preference (pp. 41, 45-47, 159, 189).

**Illustrations, layout, typography**
- Use pictures that make people curious. Before-and-after pictures increase sales (p. 40).
- One simple picture with a story; irrelevant images hurt (pp. 40-41, 115).
- Order: picture, then headline, then copy. People look at the picture first and then read, so a headline under the picture gets more readers (pp. 41, 159-160).
- Editorial-style ads beat ad-looking ads (pp. 42, 159). No all-caps or reverse type (p. 42). Clutter lowers response (p. 179).
- [not in this file] In the original, captions under photos are read more than body copy, so every caption should sell.

**TV and video** (ch. 8; pp. 45-48, 110-111, 161, 178-179)
- Works: problem-solution, demonstration, real-user testimonial, news, talking head (new products), humor (pp. 46-47). Weak: celebrities, musical vignettes (pp. 47-48).
- Tips (p. 48): brand early and repeated; open on a gripping image; spoken promises also on screen; on-camera beats voice-over; few cuts; show the product in use and its result; end on the product.
- Viewers often credit an ad to a competitor (p. 111), so name the brand more than once.
- Direct-response TV lessons (pp. 178-179): problem, then solution, then a money-back guarantee, then clear ordering instructions. Longer spots engage more.

**Direct response and testing** (ch. 11-12; pp. 62-63, 67, 115-116, 176-180)
- "In direct mail, testing is the name of the game" (p. 115). Test one variable at a time against a proven "control" (pp. 176, 179-180).
- Price and payment terms decide results, and they can be tested (p. 116).
- A narrower offer can outsell the full one. A mailing that offered only the silver coins sold more complete sets than a mailing that offered the complete set (p. 115).
- Test premiums (free extras) to see whether they bring customers who stay, not just a burst of sign-ups (p. 177).
- Make responding easy: give the number, the link and the next step (pp. 62, 115).
- Track inquiries by medium (p. 63). Show the reader how to calculate savings (p. 114).

**Research** (ch. 15; pp. 76-77, 190-194)
- Split-run tests find the winning promise (p. 192).
- People give answers that make them look good. Ogilvy's example is people claiming to have read *Gone With the Wind* (p. 193). What people do beats what they say.
- Promotions give short bumps, not loyalty (pp. 81, 197).
- "The best way to increase the sale of a product is to improve the product" (p. 127).
- Ads can sell a bad product once, not twice (pp. 93, 213).

## 2. Applications to Money X-Ray

**Store page (phone first, Stan Store), top to bottom**
1. **Picture first.** A cropped screenshot of the sample report showing the contrast: "+$1,500/mo left over on paper" next to "Visa balance up, $122/mo interest." This is a before-and-after (p. 40) and tells a story at a glance.
2. **Headline directly under the picture** (pp. 159-160). It includes "Money X-Ray," names the reader, and is specific.
3. **Subhead.** What it is and how it works, in one line. Say "the AI you already use, free plans work" early, because that answers the first objection.
4. **One CTA button with the price.** Then the screenshots, each with a caption that sells.
5. **Long copy below the fold**, with subheads (p. 160). Phone readers who scroll this far are the interested ones; facts sell to them (p. 110). Suggested subheads: What it finds / What you need (statements, 20 minutes) / What it won't do / Your privacy / Not financial advice / Guarantee / FAQ. Repeat the CTA after the FAQ.
6. **Editorial look** (p. 42): dark text on light, no all-caps or white-on-color paragraphs, short phone-sized paragraphs.
7. **One promise** (p. 74): "see exactly where your money goes." The debt plan and Notion dashboard come second.

**Demo** (pp. 46, 48, 111). Show it working: a screen recording of pasting the prompt, uploading a CSV, and the report coming back with the $0.00 balance check. The visible end result is the proof. Run the founder's voice over the screen, with numbers as on-screen text.

**30-second mid-video promo** (problem-solution with the founder on camera)
- 0-3s: The statement image and the line "Good income, still broke by the 25th?" Say "Money X-Ray" by second 5.
- 3-15s: Founder on camera, sample family story, two numbers on screen.
- 15-25s: A demo clip of the upload and report. Super: "No app. No bank login. One-time price."
- 25-30s: The product shot, the store link, and how to buy ("link in description / pinned comment"). Say the brand twice (p. 111). Use few cuts and no jingle (p. 48).

**Shorts/Reels.** One finding per Short, framed as news (p. 110): "4 streamers + 2 music apps," "the $45 charge nobody recognized," "62 months vs 23 months." Open on the statement image, put the numbers on screen, end on the product name. Use the founder rather than a borrowed celebrity or influencer (p. 45).

**What to test first** (one variable at a time, pp. 176, 192)
1. **The headline/hook.** Use Shorts hooks as a cheap split test, then carry the winning promise onto the store page. Stan Store has no native A/B, so alternate headlines by week or use two links tagged by source.
2. **Price and terms** (p. 116), for example $39.99 against a lower number. Measure buyers, not clicks.
3. **Core-only versus bundle.** Ogilvy's silver-coins result (p. 115) suggests testing the audit alone against the audit plus bonuses.
4. **Track sales by source** (YouTube, Shorts, Reels, email) (p. 63).
5. **Ask buyers what the report found** (actual findings beat stated intentions, p. 193); with permission these become testimonials (p. 46).

## 3. Draft copy

**Headlines**
1. Money X-Ray: see where your paycheck actually goes, using the AI you already have
2. Good income, still broke by the 25th? Money X-Ray reads your bank statements and shows you why
3. New: Money X-Ray audits 3 months of your own statements. No app, no bank login, no monthly fee
4. How many streaming services are you paying for? Money X-Ray counts them from your statements
5. In a sample family's statements, Money X-Ray found $340 in fees and interest in 3 months
6. Money X-Ray lists every subscription, price increase and duplicate charge, checked to $0.00
7. Before you pay for another budgeting app, see what Money X-Ray finds in the statements you already have
8. Money X-Ray turns your statements into a debt payoff plan: avalanche and snowball, side by side

**Subhead**
Paste one prompt into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini (free plans work). Upload your bank and card statements. Get a plain-English report of where your money went and what it is costing you. One-time purchase.

**Screenshot captions** (each followed by "Sample data: fictional family")
1. Balance check: every transaction accounted for, down to $0.00.
2. Subscriptions found: 4 video streamers and 2 music services. One had raised its price.
3. A $45 monthly charge nobody could name, flagged for review.
4. Fees and interest over 3 months: $340. The Visa alone cost about $122 a month.
5. Debt plan: debt-free in 23 months instead of 62, with about $5,500 less interest (if the plan is followed).

**Body copy**
Money X-Ray is a set of instructions for the AI you already use. Download your last three months of bank and credit card statements as CSV or PDF files, add any cash spending, and paste in the prompt. The AI goes through every line. First it checks that the totals balance to $0.00, so you know nothing was skipped. Then it lists where the money went by category. It flags subscriptions, price increases, fees, interest, duplicate charges and accounts you may have forgotten about. If you carry card debt, it lays out two payoff plans, avalanche and snowball, with the months and interest for each.

There is no app to install and no bank login to hand over. You pay once.

When we ran it on a sample family earning $7,290 a month, it found $340 in fees and interest over three months and a credit card they had stopped thinking about. Your results will be different. Money X-Ray is an educational tool, not financial advice. The AI can make mistakes, so the report shows its working for you to check.

## 4. Warnings for honest finance marketing

- **"Promise, large promise is the soul of an advertisement"** (p. 119, quoting Samuel Johnson) and the benefit-headline data push toward savings promises. Our rule wins: promise to show, never promise to save. Every figure from the sample family must be labeled as sample data next to the number, not in a footnote. A headline like #5 is acceptable only with "sample" in it.
- **Before-and-after visuals** (p. 40) imply a typical result. Present them as one sample case and add "your results will differ" next to them.
- **"Show the reader how to calculate the money your product would save him"** (p. 114). A calculator is fine; a projected saving per buyer is not.
- **The money-back guarantee** comes from direct-response TV (p. 179). Use it only if it is actually honored without friction, and state the terms plainly.
- **Testimonials** (p. 46): real, consenting buyers only, personal details redacted (per project memory). Never invented or composite.
- **"Rooms too small"** (p. 122) manufactures an impression of success. No fake "only 12 left," countdowns or inflated buyer counts.
- **Ogilvy defends buying for pleasure** (p. 127). For buyers who feel broke, avoid impulse and pressure tactics.
- **Premiums** (p. 177): list bonuses without a fake "$500 value."
- **Source caveat:** wording here is often Bookey's paraphrase; check the original before quoting Ogilvy publicly.
