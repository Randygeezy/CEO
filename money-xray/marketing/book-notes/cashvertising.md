# Cashvertising (Drew Eric Whitman, 2009): notes for Money X-Ray

Page numbers are the book's printed pages.

## 1. Core principles

**Life-Force 8** (p.21): built-in desires that outsell everything else: (1) survival and enjoying life, (2) food and drink, (3) freedom from fear, pain and danger, (4) sex, (5) comfortable living, (6) being superior and keeping up with the Joneses, (7) care and protection of loved ones, (8) social approval. Desire is tension; tension drives action (p.24). People also enjoy *reading about* someone else satisfying a want, and "the first use of any product is inside the consumers' minds" (p.25). The job: make them picture using it, then prove it works, then push them to act (p.26-27).

**9 learned wants** (p.23): be informed, curiosity, cleanliness, efficiency, convenience, dependability/quality, beauty/style, economy/profit, bargains. These are real but much weaker than the LF8.

**17 psychology principles (ch. 2):**
- **Fear** (p.29-34): it works through stress. It only works when all four parts are there: a real threat, a specific fix, a fix that feels effective, and a buyer who believes they can do it (p.31). Too much fear freezes people (p.32). Use fears people already have; don't invent new ones (p.32). He calls it ethical only if the product really solves the problem (p.30-31).
- **Ego morphing** (p.34-37): buyer adopts the product's identity. **Transfer** (p.38-40): borrow trust from respected institutions. **Bandwagon** (p.40-43): aspirational, associative and dissociative groups.
- **Means-end chain** (p.44-45): sell the "benefit of the benefit" (the hole, not the shovel).
- **Transtheoretical model** (p.46-47): unaware → contemplating → preparing → buying → habit.
- **Inoculation** (p.47-50): raise a weak counter-argument, help the reader beat it; consumer-advocate stance. **Belief re-ranking** (p.50-54): build on existing beliefs.
- **Elaboration likelihood** (p.54-57): high-stakes purchases (money is one) need the "central route" of facts and proof; cheap ones run on cues.
- **Cialdini's CLARCCS** (p.57-64): comparison, liking, authority, reciprocation (give a real gift first, p.60-62), commitment/consistency (a run of yes-questions, p.62-63), scarcity (p.63-64).
- **Clarity** (p.64), **examples beat statistics** (p.65-67), **two-sided messages**, even saying who *shouldn't* buy (p.67-69), **repetition with variation** (p.69-70), **rhetorical questions** (evidence mixed, p.70-72), **evidence** from outside sources (p.72-74), **length-implies-strength** (p.75-78).

**41 ad-agency secrets (ch. 3), the ones that matter here:**
- Simplicity: aim for about 11-word sentences and mostly one-syllable words. Be concrete: "$2,495 a week" beats "financially successful" (#1, p.80-89).
- Load the copy with benefits, not features (#2, p.89-92).
- Put the biggest benefit in the headline. About 60% of people read only the headline, and readership drops after about 12 words (#3, p.92-96).
- 22 headline starters: Free, New, At Last, Warning, Here's, How, Do You, If You... (#5, p.99-101). 12 ways to open body copy (#6, p.101-103).
- **Extreme specificity**: whoever explains their process wins, even if competitors do the same thing (#9, p.106-109).
- Picture, then headline, then copy; captions get about twice the readership of body copy (#10, p.110).
- Type: on screens use a sans serif at 12pt or larger, 14pt for older readers. Use initial caps, not all caps. Headlines in black on white (#11, p.111-116).
- Be personal; put ease and speed in the first sentence (#14, p.118-126).
- Social proof: ask customers for testimonials and get written permission (#15, p.126-127).
- Show a face looking at the reader; a quote headline next to it (#16, p.127-128).
- Powerful visual adjectives (#17) and mental movies that use sight, sound, feeling, smell and taste (#18, p.128-137).
- Fight inertia: make buying easy, then ask (#19, p.137-139).
- A truthful unique selling proposition (#20, p.139-143).
- Authority through free reports (#22, p.144-147); test offers and survey the audience (#27-28, p.158-161).
- Use pictures. Show the product in use: +13% (#24, p.149-152).
- Long copy outsells short copy, online too (#26, p.154-157).
- Guarantees: longer, stronger guarantees bring *fewer* returns. Display it with a border and your signature (#33, p.169-171).
- Blue is the most liked color; black on white reads best (#37, p.175-178).
- Pricing: prices ending in 9 or 99 signal value; round numbers signal quality (#38, p.178-180).
- White space (#40, p.182); "Cleverectomy": clear beats clever (#41, p.182-185).

Ch. 4 (p.187-192): checklists for response, value, easy buying, and a 46-point ad checklist.

## 2. Applications to Money X-Ray

**Which LF8 desires our buyer feels**
- **#3 freedom from fear/pain**, the main one: the dread of checking the balance, the Visa that keeps growing, interest, surprise charges.
- **#7 care for loved ones**: "the family's money," kids, a partner who is tired of fighting about it.
- **#5 comfortable living**: money actually left at the end of the month.
- **#6 winning**: beating the card company, not "paying them $122 a month for nothing."
- **#8 social approval**: from "bad with money" to "has a plan." Gently; shame freezes people.
- Learned: *curiosity* (that $45 charge), *being informed*, *convenience* (no app, no bank linking), *economy* (no monthly fee).
- Means-end chain: the real product is the calm feeling of knowing, not "a prompt." Selling a prompt is the shovel; selling that calm is the hole.

**Using fear honestly (debt, interest)**
- Keep three of his four fear steps and drop the first. Don't try to scare people badly. Show a real, specific, widely known threat with honest numbers (23% APR, about $122/month of interest in the sample family). Then right away: the specific fix, proof that it works (the sample report), and proof that they can do it (paste, upload, read).
- Never leave a scary number on screen without the next step beside it (p.32: fear without a way out freezes people).
- The fear should come from *their own statements*, not from our ad. "Your numbers will tell you" is honest. "You're probably drowning" is not.
- Two-sided message (p.67-69): "A budgeting app is great if you're fine linking your bank and paying monthly. If you're not, here's another way." Also say who it's *not* for: people who want someone to manage their money for them.

**Social proof before we have customers**
- **Demonstration over testimonials** (p.151): show the sample family's full report, clearly labeled as a fictional test case. A real worked result beats invented praise.
- **Length-implies-strength, used honestly** (p.75-77): list everything it checks. Balance reconciled to $0.00, subscriptions, price increases, fees, interest, duplicates, forgotten accounts, avalanche vs. snowball. Every item must be real.
- **Founder face and voice** (p.127, p.144-147): the channel's videos are the "free reports."
- **Reciprocation** (p.60-62): give away a real free mini-check (a "find your forgotten subscriptions" prompt).
- **Survey and beta** (p.126, p.159): ask channel viewers to run it and send honest, permissioned quotes. Show them with first name and city only, and with consent. Follow the memory rule: redact personal info from real statements.
- **Transfer, carefully**: avalanche and snowball are named, established methods; never imply any agency or bank endorses us.
- Bandwagon only with true numbers.

**Layout for a phone store page**
- First screen: a headline of 12 words or fewer with the biggest benefit (p.92-96), the founder's face looking at the camera (p.127), and one screenshot of the report on a phone with a selling caption (p.110, p.151).
- Short sentences, about 11 words, plain words (p.83-87). Sans serif, 16px or larger on phones (his 14pt advice for older readers). Dark text on white, no reversed paragraphs (p.104-106, p.116). Initial caps in headlines (p.114).
- Long scroll is fine if broken up with selling subheads and bullets (p.154-157, p.190-191).
- One product, one button; "It's easy:" plus 3 steps (p.138-139, p.190).
- Guarantee in a bordered box with a signature, near the button (p.171).
- Price: $39.99 reads as value (p.179). Break it down only with honest comparisons ("less than a month of the sample family's card interest").

## 3. Draft copy

**10 hooks for Shorts/Reels**
1. "This family had $1,500 left over every month. So why did their Visa keep growing?"
2. "There's a $45 charge in these statements. Nobody in the house knows what it is."
3. "Four video streamers. Two music apps. One family. Nobody noticed."
4. "This family pays their credit card company $122 a month for nothing. Here's where it shows."
5. "Debt-free in 23 months instead of 62. Same income. Same bills. Different order."
6. "Don't link your bank to another app. Do this instead."
7. "Netflix raised the price. Would you have caught it in your statement?"
8. "The AI found a credit card this family forgot they had."
9. "If you earn good money and still feel broke on the 28th, watch this."
10. "$340 in fees and interest in three months. Here's how to find yours."

(All facts from the labeled sample family: examples and specificity, p.65-67, p.106-109.)

**5 headlines**
1. "Find Out Where Your Money Really Goes, Without Linking Your Bank"
2. "Earn Good Money but Still Feel Broke? Your Statements Know Why."
3. "At Last: A Line-by-Line Audit of Your Statements, Done by the AI You Already Use"
4. "How a Sample Family Found $340 in Fees and Interest in 3 Months of Statements"
5. "Warning: Credit Card Interest May Be Eating the Money You Think Is 'Left Over'"

**Benefit bullets**
- See every dollar from the last few months sorted into plain categories, checked against your balance down to $0.00.
- Spot the subscriptions you forgot: the second music app, the trial that turned into a bill.
- Catch quiet price increases before they repeat for years.
- Put a number on what fees and interest really cost you, in dollars per month, not just a percentage.
- Find the duplicate charge, the mystery charge and the account you forgot you had.
- Get a payoff order, avalanche or snowball, with a month to circle on the calendar.
- No bank linking, no app, no monthly fee. Use the AI you already have; free plans work.
- Plain-English results you can show your partner at the kitchen table.

**Guarantee / risk reversal** (the terms are the founder's call)

> **My promise:** Run Money X-Ray on your own statements. If it doesn't show you at least one thing about your money you didn't know, email me within [30] days and I'll refund every cent. No forms, no questions. I can't promise you'll save a set amount, because that depends on your choices. I can promise you'll see your numbers clearly. *[signature]*

A longer guarantee brings fewer returns, not more (p.170).

## 4. Warnings: what not to borrow for a finance product

- **"Scare the hell out of people"** (p.31, quoting Pratkanis & Aronson). Germ, mite and spider copy (p.29-30, p.132-133). Creating new fears the way Listerine did with "halitosis" (p.33). For people already anxious about debt, this is manipulation and also backfires (p.32). Stay calm.
- **Fake scarcity and deadlines.** He says to "always feature deadlines" and gives stock phrases such as "Supplies Are Strictly Limited" (p.97-98), plus invented "only 3 more clients" lines (p.64). A digital product has unlimited supply. Use only true deadlines, such as a real launch price with a real end date that we actually keep.
- **Hidden persuasion.** "Prospects must remain unaware that you're attempting to influence them" (p.53). Repeating "you learned" until readers doubt their own judgment (p.89). We want the opposite: openly educational.
- **Ads disguised as news** (p.162-163): FTC risk; label sponsored content.
- **Meaningless borrowed authority**: unknown "guild" logos (p.39-40), actors dressed as doctors (p.59-60). No fake badges.
- **Length-implies-strength abused**: the politician's "200 flip-flops," mostly taken out of context (p.77). Every item in our feature list has to be true.
- **Income/result claims** ("You'll make up to $2,495 every week," p.85; "Rake in $2,750 cash weekly!" p.128). We never promise savings or 100% accuracy. The sample family's results must be labeled as a fictional example, not typical results.
- **Selling credit to the debt-stressed**: "Bill me" options and installments to dull the pain of spending (p.139, p.188). Off-brand for us.
- **Clickbait hooks** get clicks, not sales (p.168).
- **Ego morphing through inadequacy** (p.34) and insinuations about competitors (p.171). No shaming, factual comparisons only.
