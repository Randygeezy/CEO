## 1. Core principles

### The One Belief (16-Word, pp21-28)
A letter has one mission: make the reader believe that *this new opportunity is the key to their desire, and it's attainable only through my new mechanism* (p23).
- **Opportunity:** what's new, your USP. If you can't name one, the idea isn't worth testing (pp23-24).
- **Desire:** the benefit the reader already wants.
- **Mechanism:** *how* it works. Markets that have heard every claim need "a new way to make the old promise work" (Schwartz, p25).

The One Belief is the common thread that holds the letter together (p19).

### The ten questions, in order (pp29-92)
1. **How is this different?** Novelty gets attention. Put the USP in the headline (pp29-33).
2. **What's in it for me?** Make the big promise on page one. Phone readers are looking for a reason to leave (pp34-38).
3. **How do I know it's real?** Give early proof, framed as **And/But/Therefore** story rather than a list of facts. Adding statistics to a story *lowered* donations (pp39-47).
4. **What's holding me back?** Reverse question 1 to reveal the "real problem." This lifts blame off the reader and gives hope. It must rest on facts (pp48-53).
5. **Who/what is to blame?** A common enemy, built from beliefs the reader already holds (pp54-60).
6. **Why now?** Beat inertia. He teaches this as an "either-or" false dilemma (pp61-65).
7. **Why trust you?** "I've been in your shoes" (strongest), Robin Hood, or Expert. Questions 1 to 7 fill the first third of the letter (pp66-73).
8. **How does it work?** Explain the mechanism so the reader nods, ideally using something they already believe (pp74-78).
9. **How do I get started?** A no-brainer offer with a wide gap between value and price: purpose-built bonuses, a value stack, a guarantee (pp79-83).
10. **What do I have to lose?** Push-pull close: three options, no neediness, and the cost of standing still (pp84-90).

Before drafting, sketch the belief and questions 1 to 7 in a few lines each (p91).

### Great Leads (Bookey summary)
- **The lead does most of the selling.** By the 80/20 rule, the first 20% of copy carries about 80% of the emotional impact (PDF pp8-12, bk 3-19).
- **Rule of One:** one big idea, presented clearly, beats a dozen (PDF pp14-19, bk 22-36).
- **Start by asking what the customer already knows.** Schwartz's ladder: Most aware → Product-aware → Solution-aware → Problem-aware → Unaware. Bookey cuts off at problem-aware (PDF pp20-21, bk 39-55).
- **Direct vs indirect:** the more aware the reader, the more direct the lead. Go indirect for strangers, for products that need explaining, or for skeptics, before they file you under "heard that before" (PDF pp23-26, 56, bk 58-74).

**The six leads, from most direct to most indirect:**

| Lead | Fits | Rules |
|---|---|---|
| Offer | Most aware | Lead with the best detail of the deal and its value, plus a *reason why*. Use "FREE" sparingly (PDF pp27-31, bk 77-98) |
| Promise | Product-aware / mostly aware | Biggest benefit tied to a core desire. Original, bold but believable, proved fast. Scale it down for skeptics (pp32-33, 85-90, bk 101-120) |
| Problem-Solution | Problem-aware | "What keeps them up at night?" Show empathy for the *unspoken* worry, stay on it briefly, then turn to hope. Formats: question, if-then, negative promise (pp35-38, bk 123-140) |
| Big Secret | Solution-unaware, curious | The "velvet pouch": an intriguing, useful secret in the headline, not revealed in the lead, with clues along the way (pp39-41, bk 143-183) |
| Proclamation | Unaware or jaded | A bold, specific statement written like a news story, proved right after and repeated at the close (pp42-44, 98-103, bk 186-205) |
| Story | Least aware, most skeptical | "Sneak into the psyche sideways." Example: the two-classmates letter, which ran 28 years (pp45-47, bk 208-229) |


**The golden thread** (PDF p105) is the line that ties the product to the prospect's emotion and keeps them reading to the sale. [gen] In the full book, it's one idea running unbroken from headline to order button, which is the same thing as the One Belief. Our thread for every hook, promo, page, email and report: **"You're not bad with money. You've just never seen it all in one place."**

## 2. Applied to Money X-Ray (draft)

**One Belief:** *Seeing every real transaction from every account, checked to $0.00 (opportunity), is the key to learning why you feel broke on a decent income and what to fix first (desire). The easiest way to get there is the Money X-Ray prompt, which turns the AI you already use into a line-by-line auditor, with no app, no bank login and no monthly fee (mechanism).* I softened "only through" on purpose: a spreadsheet could do this too.

**Ten answers:**
1. **Different:** it audits your *actual statements*, not budget guesses, inside ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini (free plans too). It never links to your bank.
2. **What's in it:** in one sitting, see where the money goes, what you forgot you pay for, what fees and interest cost, and which debt to pay first.
3. **Real:** the labelled fictional demo audit, sample-report screenshots and the $0.00 balance check. Real customer stories only once they exist, with permission.
4. **Holding back:** budgets and apps look forward. Leaks sit in past statements, scattered across several cards, and nobody reads them line by line.
5. **Blame:** systems, not people. Set-and-forget subscription billing, quiet price rises, fees, 23% card interest, and apps that charge monthly to show you your own money.
6. **Why now:** the honest cost of waiting. In the demo, interest is about $122 a month, and every billing cycle repeats the leaks. No fake deadline.
7. **Trust:** [founder's true "I've been in your shoes" story], the channel's track record, and plain disclaimers.
8. **How:** download statements, paste the prompt and upload, then get the audit and a debt plan (avalanche vs snowball). Optional Notion dashboard.
9. **Start:** $39.99 one-time (price not final) on Stan Store. Bonuses only if they actually ship. Guarantee is for the owner to decide.
10. **Lose:** do nothing, do it by hand, or run it tonight. The same charges hit next month either way.

**Lead choice:**
- **(a) YouTube viewers who know the founder:** they're product-aware and trusting, so use a **Promise lead** in the founder's voice, with the offer right behind it.
- **(b) Cold Reels viewers:** they're problem-aware ("good income, still broke") but solution-unaware, so use a **Problem-Solution lead** opened as a question, with one striking demo finding inside 3 seconds. A/B test it against a Story lead.

## 4. Warnings
- **Fake urgency.** He teaches "why now" as a false dilemma, credited to Hitler (pp61-65), and closes with invented scarcity: "one of the 250 people" (p89). Bookey's Great Leads also suggests countdowns (PDF p83). We use none of it.
- **Enemies.** His enemies are partisan ("Democrats and the corrupt liberal media," p59). Our enemy is a billing practice, never a group of people.
- **"Not your fault."** Use it to lift shame (pp48-53), not to deny that spending choices matter.
- **Belief over truth.** He holds up Brinkley's goat-gland quackery as a model (pp74-78). Every mechanism claim we make must be literally true.
- **Hype offers.** Inflated value stacks, false closes and anchors (pp80-83) are out. No made-up "$497 value." No promised savings or 100% accuracy.
- **Testimonials.** His are aliases making income claims (pp17-18). The FTC requires real, typical results. Until we have customers, use only the labelled demo.
- **Push-pull.** It comes from pickup-artist tactics (pp84-90). Keep the three options; drop the contempt in "dreamers vs doers."
- **Great Leads.** "Read This or Die"-style proclamations and fear campaigns (PDF pp48-49, 99) can tip into money-anxiety scare copy. A Secret lead must pay off honestly: say plainly that the product is a prompt.
- **Open decisions.** Price, guarantee terms, and whether the budget module exists at launch. Also tell buyers that their uploads go to their AI provider, under that provider's privacy terms.
