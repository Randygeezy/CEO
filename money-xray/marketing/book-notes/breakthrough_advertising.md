# Breakthrough Advertising (Eugene Schwartz, 1966) — notes for Money X-Ray

Page numbers are the book's printed pages (Boardroom edition). Read in full (a few pages are scanned ad images only).

## 1. Core principles

**Mass desire (pp. 3-12).** Copy cannot create desire. It can only take a want that millions already have and point it at one product. Pick the single strongest desire your product can serve, judged on urgency, staying power and how many people share it (pp. 7-8). Only one desire fits in a headline (p. 12). People buy the "functional product" (what it does), never the physical one. Physical facts give an old claim "a fresh new basis for believability" (p. 10).

**Headline's job (pp. 14-15).** It doesn't have to sell. It has to get the next sentence read.

**Five levels of awareness (pp. 15-36).**
1. *Most aware*: knows the product and wants it. Give the name, the price and the offer (p. 16).
2. *Product-aware, not yet wanting it*: show it's better, with new proof or a new mechanism (pp. 16-19).
3. *Desire-aware*: wants the result but doesn't know your product exists. Name the desire, prove it can be met, show the mechanism lives in your product (pp. 19-21).
4. *Problem-aware*: feels a need but doesn't link it to you. Name the need, dramatize it, present the solution (pp. 21-23).
5. *Unaware*: won't admit the problem. Leave price, product name and function out of the headline. Call the market together with an identification headline that echoes their feeling, then lead them down the page (pp. 23-26). Rule: "Your prospect must identify with your headline before he can buy from it," and it must turn away as many people as it pulls in (p. 35). Closest model for us: the TV-repair book, where "Save $100 on repairs" failed and "Why haven't TV owners been told these facts?" won by using an existing resentment (pp. 30-31, 135-143).

**Five stages of sophistication (pp. 37-50).**
1. *First*: state the claim plainly.
2. *Second*: make the claim bigger and measure it ("47 pounds in 4 weeks").
3. *Third*: claims are worn out, so lead with a new mechanism, "a new way to make the old promise work" (p. 42). The headline is mostly mechanism, and the claim moves into the lead.
4. *Fourth*: make that mechanism easier, faster or surer (p. 44).
5. *Fifth*: the market believes nothing, so switch to identification with the prospect (p. 45).

**Headline construction (pp. 51-58, 59-67).** "Verbalization" makes a claim stronger. Schwartz lists 38 ways, e.g. measure it, remove limits ("without surgery"), name the problem, warn of a pitfall, case history, paradox, connect mechanism to claim, challenge a limiting belief. Those 5-10 words carry about 90% of the ad's value (p. 67).

**Intensification (pp. 77-106).** Fill a vague desire with concrete scenes: the result, it in action, the reader in it, a test, benefits over time, comparison, the dark side, ease, a summary "catalog," the guarantee. Use only images you can "legitimately present" (p. 78). Short media: one central idea, freshly disguised each time (pp. 101-106).

**Identification (pp. 107-128).** Products also sell roles. Start from the product's existing image and build bridges from it. Don't contradict it (pp. 117-119).

**Gradualization (pp. 129-151).** "If you violate your prospect's established beliefs in the slightest degree," nothing saves the ad (p. 131). Structure builds belief: start with statements the reader already accepts and chain agreements toward the goal conclusion. "One fully-believed promise has ten times the sales power of ten partially-believed promises" (p. 144). Tools: inclusion question, accurate symptom lists (p. 146), contradicting a false belief, if/then.

**Redefinition (pp. 153-164).** Remove objections by changing what something means. *Simplification*: "repairs" became "minor adjustments" (pp. 155-160). *Escalation*: make the product matter more by showing where it pays off (pp. 160-162). *Price*: compare against a bigger standard, not the cheaper rivals (pp. 162-164).

**Mechanization (pp. 165-173).** The reader asks "Tell me more," "Who says so?" and "How does it work?" (p. 166). If the mechanism is familiar, name it. If not, describe it in selling language. In a sophisticated market, feature it in the headline (pp. 167-171).

**Concentration (pp. 175-184).** Take apart the other ways the reader could get the result, but "Never attack a weakness unless you can provide the solution" (p. 177). The best weapon is a better product: "if your copy is better than your product," fix the product (p. 176).

**Camouflage (pp. 185-194).** Borrow believability from a medium's format. Better: *Understatement* (VW) and *Deadly Sincerity*, admitting flaws so strengths are believed (pp. 192-194).

**Final touches (pp. 195-225).** Place proof where the reader demands it (pp. 196-200); pair claims so they multiply (Avis, p. 200); open loops for momentum (pp. 215-222).

## 2. Diagnosis for Money X-Ray

**Awareness**
- *YouTube storytelling viewer (warm)*: at Level 3-4 when the promo plays. The story has just made the problem vivid ("I earn decent money, where does it go?"). They want the result but don't know a product does it. Lead with the desire or problem, then the mechanism, then the product name. Don't open on price.
- *Shorts/Reels stranger (cold)*: at Level 4-5. Many won't admit "I'm bad with money" and blame prices. Use identification and resentment ("you make good money and it still disappears"), not product, price or "budget." Turn away people who don't earn decent money.
- *Store page visitor*: Level 2-3. Needs mechanism, proof (demo report), offer.

**Sophistication.** "Find your subscriptions / make a budget" is at **Stage 4 going on 5**. Apps already promise to cancel subscriptions and "save $X a year"; free AI prompts are everywhere; savings claims get discounted. So:
- Bigger claims ("find $500!") are dead and dishonest anyway. Lead with a **new mechanism** (Stage 3/4 move) and pair it with **identification** for cold traffic (Stage 5 move).
- The real point of difference sits in how the product works: (a) the audit **balances to $0.00**, so every dollar on every statement is accounted for and nothing hides in an "other" bucket; (b) it runs on **the AI you already have, with your statements, no bank login**; (c) it's a **one-time** purchase.

**Named mechanism: "The $0.00 Check"** (long form: "the $0.00 Balance Check"). The audit isn't finished until the money in and out of your statements reconciles to zero difference. Anything left over gets named: fees, interest, duplicate charges, price increases, a forgotten card. It's true of the prompt and easy to demo. Keep the wording to "accounted for in the statements you upload," not "100% accurate."

**Redefinitions to use**
- *Simplification*: from "budgeting" (hard, failed before) to "paste one prompt, upload last 3 months, read the report."
- *Price*: compare one $39.99 purchase with a budgeting app's yearly fee, not with free prompts.

## 3. Concrete applications

**10 headline drafts** (W = warm/page, C = cold hook)
1. (W, mechanism) "Every dollar in your statements, accounted for to $0.00. Then you'll see where it went."
2. (W, mechanism + limits removed) "Find the subscriptions, fees and interest in your statements. No bank login, no monthly fee."
3. (W, new + metaphor) "An X-ray for your bank statements, run by the AI you already use."
4. (W, inclusion) "Take home $5,000+ a month and still check your balance before you buy groceries? Read this."
5. (C, identification) "Good income. Nothing left by the 25th. You're not bad with money. You've just never seen the whole list."
6. (C, resentment, TV-ad structure) "Nobody sends you a letter when a subscription goes up $3."
7. (C, case history, sample family) "The $45 charge nobody in the house could explain."
8. (W, paradox, sample family) "$1,500 left over every month, on paper. So why did the Visa keep growing?"
9. (W, pitfall warning) "Before you download another budgeting app, read last month's statements this way."
10. (W, contradict limiting belief) "You don't need a budget yet. You need to know where the money went."

Use hooks 7-8 only with "sample family / test data" on screen.

**Sales page opening** (gradualization: inclusion, accurate symptoms, then the mechanism, with the claim held back):

> If your household brings home good money and still feels tight by the end of the month, this page is for you.
> You've probably looked at your bank app. You've seen the big bills: mortgage, car, groceries. They don't explain it.
> What usually explains it are the small things you never see all at once: a streaming price that went up, a $9 app you forgot, a card that charges interest every month, a fee you didn't notice.
> Money X-Ray puts them all in one place. You paste one prompt into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini (free plans work), upload your statements, and the audit runs **The $0.00 Check**: it doesn't finish until every dollar in those statements is accounted for. Whatever's left over gets named.
> Here's what that looked like for a sample family…  *[show demo report]*

Then: how it works → fair comparison with bank-linking subscription apps → debt plan over time → "what you get" catalog → guarantee → price.

**30-second mid-video promo** (one desire, one mechanism, warm viewer):
- 0-5s, bridge from the story: "That's exactly what happens when good money disappears without a list."
- 5-15s, mechanism demo on screen: statements uploaded → "$0.00 difference" → findings appear (sample family: 6 streaming/music services, a $45 unknown charge, $340 in fees and interest in 3 months).
- 15-22s, simplification: "No app. No bank login. Use the AI you already have, free plan works."
- 22-27s, offer: one-time price, what's included, "educational, not financial advice."
- 27-30s, call to action: "Link in the description."

Label demo data as sample data on screen. If footage of the founder's real statements is used, redact every identifier first (from project memory).

**Campaign**: fixed central idea ("The $0.00 Check"); rotate the finding each Short opens on (pp. 103-104).

**Voice**: Understatement and Deadly Sincerity: say plainly what it won't do (no bank link, no advice, no promised savings).

## 4. Warnings (where the book pushes toward hype or deception)

- **Stage-2 "enlarge the claim" (pp. 39-41)**: Schwartz admits these were "pushed to the outer limits of both legality and believability." Don't use savings numbers as headlines. The demo results are one fictional household, so label them that way and never imply they're typical.
- **"Building terrifying fear" (Preface, p. vii) and hidden-fear/shame ads (Postum, Listerine, pp. 27-34)**: don't shame money habits or inflate debt fear. Use the resentment angle (fees, quiet price rises), which is true and not degrading.
- **Camouflage (pp. 185-191)** suggests dressing ads as news, bylined reports, or mail that looks like refund checks or government letters. **Don't.** That's deceptive under FTC rules. Never imitate a bank, IRS or news format. Disclose that it's paid or self-promotional in videos.
- **Price "reason-why" (pp. 162-164, 171-173)**: the "practically handmade, would sell for $5" and overstock stories invent a higher reference price. No fake "was $99" prices, no fake countdowns or "only 50 left" on a digital product.
- **Guarantee as climax (pp. 99-100)**: those guarantees promised results ("9 miles more per gallon or your money back"). Ours must not promise savings or accuracy. Guarantee only what we control (for example, a refund if it doesn't work on your statements, within X days).
- **Testimonials and experts (pp. 89-91, 183)**: the lecithin and "astonished experts" passages are made-up authority. Use real buyer quotes only, with permission, and no implied endorsement by finance professionals.
- **Character roles "subject to fantasy" (p. 112)**: sell the real outcome (knowing your numbers), not a fantasy identity.
- **Logic words (p. 148) and teasers (p. 219)**: they persuade "regardless of the content"; use them only where true.
- In his favor: he says desire can't be invented, belief can't be violated, detailed symptoms "must be accurate," and a better product beats better copy (pp. 3, 131, 146, 176). He lists tactics "not as recommendations" (p. 50).
