# Wundt, *Principles of Physiological Psychology*: notes for Money X-Ray

## 1. What this book actually is

The file is **Volume I only** of Titchener's 1904 translation of Wundt's 5th German edition (1902). It covers the Introduction and Part I, "The Bodily Substrate of the Mental Life" (pp. 1-333, plus index). Titchener's preface says so (p. xi). The chapters people know Wundt for are in Volumes II and III, and **they are not in this text**: intensity of sensation and Weber's law, visual form and colour, the three-way theory of feeling, attention and apperception in full, association, and reaction time. "Weber" and "reaction time" never appear in the file. The page references below are therefore limited to what Volume I says. Where I use ideas from the later volumes I mark them **[not in this file]** and give no page.

What Volume I covers:
- **Introduction (pp. 1-26):** the field's aims, experiment over armchair introspection, plan of the six Parts, faculty psychology.
- **Ch. I-III (pp. 27-131):** mind from protozoa upward; nerve cells and fibres; nerve mechanics (excitation, inhibition, reflexes, practice, fatigue).
- **Ch. IV-V (to c. p. 240):** brain embryology and conduction paths, cortical maps for dog, monkey and man.
- **Ch. VI (pp. 241-333):** brain function. Decerebrated animals, localisation and phrenology, three short case studies (visual, speech, "apperception" centres) and five general principles.

A 19th-century science textbook, roughly 85% neuroanatomy, with no persuasion or marketing in it. Usable pages: 5-6, 13-15, 28, 75-78, 299-301, 310-328.

## 2. Principles that carry over (own words, with page refs)

**Attention picks one thing and pushes the rest down (pp. 316-318, 320).** Wundt separates how *clear* something is from how *strong* it is: a faint impression can be clearly grasped, a loud one only dimly. One item becomes clear because the others are held back (p. 317). In his diagram, *a* is "apperceived" (grasped) while *b, c, d* are merely "perceived" (p. 320).
→ *For us:* making everything bigger and bolder does not make it clearer. One item gets clear when the items around it are quieter.

**Contrast is how things are judged.** Wundt says an impression is always "apprehended as clearer in contrast to other impressions" (p. 317). Vision is likewise built from each light impression's *relations* to other sensations, not from isolated points (pp. 299-300). The fuller treatment of sensory contrast is in Vol. II **[not in this file]**.

**Relative differences (Weber's law) [not in this file].** Vol. I only says sensations and feelings are measurable "intensive magnitudes" and credits Fechner (p. 6). The law: a change is noticed in proportion to its base, so $10 is obvious on $20 and invisible on $2,000.

**Feeling travels with every sensation (pp. 13-15).** Wundt splits experience into sensations (blue, warm) and feelings (pleasantness, unpleasantness, excitement, depression, p. 13). The two always occur together, and feelings can "influence sensations and ideas" as much as the reverse (p. 15). His later three dimensions (pleasant/unpleasant, tension/relaxation, excitement/calm) are in Vols. II-III **[not in this file]**.

**Feeling comes before meaning (pp. 311-312).** This is the most useful passage for writers. A word has three parts: its sound, its written form, and its meaning, and the meaning itself has an idea part and a *feeling* part (p. 311). "A heard word first of all arouses a feeling of its meaning, before the meaning itself has come clearly to consciousness" (p. 312). With unfamiliar words the process often *stops* at that vague feeling. With abstract words the idea fades and only the feeling is left (p. 311).
→ *For us:* jargon leaves the reader with a feeling and no content. "Amortization" produces a sense that this is complicated and not meant for them.

**Association, practice and fatigue (pp. 75-78, 311-312, 324-325).** Repeated stimulation makes a path easier to fire, until a weak stimulus produces a full response (pp. 75-76); unused paths decline (p. 325). Writing from dictation "slips... into a meaningless routine" as word surface crowds out meaning (p. 311). What we notice depends on the "disposition of consciousness, as determined by past experience" (p. 318).

**Action starts with feelings and ideas (p. 28).** Wundt's sign of a voluntary act is that "it is preceded by feelings and ideas" that the person takes to be its cause. A purchase is a voluntary act, so the buyer needs the idea (what this does) and the feeling (relief, control) before the button means anything.

## 3. Applications to Money X-Ray

**Phone store page (Stan Store).**
- **One clear item per screen.** Each phone-height section gets one idea (the problem, the demo, what you get, price, guarantee), and everything else on that screen is visibly quieter. Follows pp. 317 and 320.
- **Make the main item stand out by quieting the rest.** Keep one accent colour for the single number or button that matters. Grey out supporting text. Use one CTA style and do not repeat it in three colours.
- **No vague-feeling words in headlines.** Drop "financial optimization" and "holistic audit". Use concrete words that carry a clear idea and a clear feeling together: "the $45 charge you don't recognise", "a card you forgot you had". Follows p. 312.
- **Idea and feeling before the ask (p. 28):** familiar feeling (good income, broke at month end), then what the AI finds, then price.

**Presenting numbers (relative vs absolute).** The principle is that size is judged against a reference. The honest choice depends on what the reader is comparing against.
- **"$1,464 a year"** puts the cost of interest next to things people buy yearly: a flight, a car repair, the $39.99 price. Use it when setting the product price beside the leak. $39.99 against $1,464 is a big, honest contrast.
- **"$122 a month"** puts it next to the monthly budget and the bill-sized items people already track. Use it in the report, for monthly decisions.
- Show both as the same money: "$122 a month, which is $1,464 a year." Never imply the reader will *save* $1,464; it is what the demo family *paid*.
- **Small leaks:** group them ("4 streamers + 2 music apps = $X/month") so the total registers against a $7,290 take-home.
- **Debt plan:** "23 months instead of 62" lands as a comparison. "About $5,500 less interest" needs "in the demo family's case", never a promise.

**Video pacing (Shorts, Reels, 30-second promo).**
- One idea per beat. Each beat gets one on-screen number and one sentence, with nothing else competing (p. 320).
- Change against what came before. A cut, a number reveal or a shift in tone earns attention because it differs from the previous beat (p. 317). Constant loudness wears out the contrast.
- Lead with the feeling and land the meaning fast: "you earn good money and you're still broke on the 28th", then the concrete finding. Do not leave viewers on a vague feeling (p. 312).
- Repetition builds familiarity (pp. 75-76, 324): same motif, name and reveal format across Shorts.

**Report design (the AI's output and the sample report).**
- Top of the report: **one headline finding** in large type, such as "$1,500 a month is left over on paper, and your Visa still grows." Supporting findings come after it at a lower visual level.
- Use a consistent hierarchy: one accent for money leaving (fees, interest, forgotten charges) and neutral styling for everything else. The accent loses meaning if half the page uses it.
- Put each finding next to its reference: "Netflix: $15.49 → $17.99 (+16%)", "fees + interest: $340 in 3 months, more than your phone bill". Percentages and comparisons make small numbers register.
- Use plain words and gloss any finance term ("APR, the yearly interest rate on the card"), since unknown words leave readers with only a vague feeling (p. 312).
- Calm tone: aim for relief and control, not alarm.

## 4. What not to take from it

- **The neuroscience is out of date.** The "apperception centre" in the frontal lobes is, in Wundt's own words, "hypothetical" and "conjecture" (p. 318). Do not cite it as brain science in marketing ("your brain's attention centre...").
- **Localisation, phrenology and brain-comparison material** (e.g. Gauss's brain against one "of moderate intelligence", pp. 285, 315) is historical. Leave it alone.
- **Introspection**, his method, was largely dropped by later psychology. Treat his claims as plausible observations, not proven findings.
- **No neuro-marketing claims** ("scientifically proven to..."). Wundt says nothing about buying.
- **Honesty conflicts:** the book holds no hype tactics to flag. The only risk is our own: using relative framing to exaggerate. Choose the frame that matches what the reader is deciding, show the equivalent figure, and never present the demo family's numbers as the buyer's expected result.
- **For the missing topics** (Weber's law in detail, the three dimensions of feeling, association, reaction times), get Volumes II and III or the shorter *Outlines of Psychology* (1897), which Wundt names as the companion text (p. 12). Do not cite page numbers from this file for them.
